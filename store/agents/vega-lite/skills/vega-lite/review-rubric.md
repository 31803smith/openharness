# Chart review rubric

A fresh reader judges the chart image against the brief's question. Judge only what the image and the
brief show. Interactions cannot be seen in a still, so do not grade them. The chart passes only when
every criterion passes. A failing note says exactly what to change, in terms of the chart
("the x axis has no unit: title it 'Trip distance (km)'"), in one line.

### `answers-question`
The title and the marks answer the brief's question on their own, before any interaction. The main
comparison, trend or relationship is the most visible thing on the chart. The title states the question
or the finding, and matches what the data shows.

### `honest-encoding`
The encoding does not mislead. Bar and area lengths start at zero. A non-zero quantitative axis is
clearly intentional and does not exaggerate differences. Time runs left to right in order. Aggregates
say what they are (sum, mean, count). One colour keeps one meaning across every panel. Nothing implies
causation the data cannot show.

### `labels-and-units`
Every quantitative axis and legend has a title with its unit or scale (%, km, MWh, count). Numbers
and dates read naturally: no long decimals, raw timestamps or 0–1 shares shown as bare fractions when
they are meant as percentages. Categories are named in words a reader understands.

### `legible`
No text overlaps other text or marks in a way that hides it. No label, tick, legend entry, title or data
point is clipped at the edge or cut short. Axis labels are not so dense they collide. Text is large
enough to read when the image is shown at half size.

### `composition`
The layout has a clear reading order: title, then the main view, then supporting views. Panels align,
share scales where they compare the same thing, and use space in proportion to their importance.
Nothing is squeezed into a sliver, and no panel is mostly empty.

### `colour`
The palette is restrained and purposeful. Categories are easy to tell apart. Highlight colour is used
for what matters and not spent on decoration. Text and marks have enough contrast against the background.
